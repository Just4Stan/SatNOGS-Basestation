\section{The AetherSpace CubeSat}
\label{sec:aetherspace}

AetherSpace is a 3U CubeSat~\cite{cubesat-spec} developed at KU~Leuven for a heat-shield sample-return demonstration from LEO, with a planned launch around 2030. Per the specification provided by the AetherSpace team (2026-03-05): UHF downlink at 433~MHz, TX power approximately +14~dBm ($\approx$25~mW), TX antenna a turnstile or dipole with approximately 2~dBi gain, CC1200 native packet format, modulation and symbol rate not finalised at the time of writing. Hanssens (2023) on SatNOGS ground-station options~\cite{hanssens2023} provides the closest prior KU~Leuven work on the ground-station side.

The downstream sections of this chapter take the AetherSpace specification as fixed input and derive the requirements it imposes on the ground segment: the geometry of the pass that the rotator must follow (\S\ref{sec:orbital-mechanics}), the link budget against the +14~dBm/2~dBi transmitter (\S\ref{sec:link-budget}), the Doppler shift the receiver must compensate (\S\ref{sec:doppler}), and the integration path into the SatNOGS ecosystem the station has to honour (\S\ref{sec:satnogs}).

\section{LEO Orbital Mechanics}
\label{sec:orbital-mechanics}

For a circular orbit at altitude $h$ above Earth's surface, the orbital velocity follows from equating gravitational attraction to centripetal acceleration~\cite{montenbruck2000orbits}:
\begin{equation}
    v = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{R_E + h}}
\end{equation}
with $\mu = GM_\oplus = 3.986 \times 10^{14}$~m$^3$/s$^2$ and $R_E = 6371$~km. For $h = 400$~km: $v = \sqrt{3.986 \times 10^{14} / 6.771 \times 10^6} = 7.669$~km/s. The orbital period is
\begin{equation}
    T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{(R_E + h)^3}{\mu}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{(6.771 \times 10^6)^3}{3.986 \times 10^{14}}} = 5545~\text{s} = 92.4~\text{min}.
\end{equation}

The maximum slant range $d_{\max}$ at the geometric horizon (elevation $\epsilon = 0$\degree) for a spherical Earth is
\begin{equation}
    d_{\max} = \sqrt{(R_E+h)^2 - R_E^2} = \sqrt{6771^2 - 6371^2} = 2293~\text{km}.
\end{equation}

Visible-arc geometry (the angle subtended at Earth's centre by the portion of the orbit above the station horizon) gives a theoretical maximum pass duration of
\begin{equation}
    t_{\max} = T \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\pi} \approx 10.1~\text{min at } h = 400~\text{km, zenith pass,}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha = \arccos(R_E / (R_E + h))$. Passes with practical elevations above 10\degree\ are shorter, typically 5--8~min in field data (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

The total angular rate as seen from the ground station reaches its bound at zenith:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\theta}_{\max} = \frac{v}{h} = \frac{7669}{400 \times 10^3}~\text{rad/s} = 1.10~\text{deg/s}.
\end{equation}

The \textit{azimuth} rate, however, is unbounded as a pass approaches true zenith: for passes with closest-approach elevation above $\sim$70\degree, the AZ axis alone needs to move through a large arc in a short time, so instantaneous AZ rates can exceed 10\degree/s for a few seconds. This is a speed-limit concern for the mechanical rotator (\S\ref{sec:tracking-accuracy}) rather than an accuracy concern.

The satellite's radial velocity relative to the ground station also produces a Doppler shift on the received carrier frequency. Because the radial velocity changes continuously throughout the pass, the received frequency must be tracked in real time; the magnitude and correction strategy are derived in \S\ref{sec:doppler}.

Two observations follow~\cite{pratt2003satellite,roddy2006satellite}. First, even at the datasheet CC1200 sensitivity the AetherSpace link closes with positive margin only at high elevations; mid-elevation and horizon geometries are negative. Second, the field-measured noise floor at the CC1200 input sits approximately 30~dB above the expected kTB+NF limit of $-$127~dBm, attributable to board-level interference (LMR51420 buck harmonics, RP2040 digital noise, Pi GPIO emissions); at this floor the received signal of $-$125.7~dBm is buried well below the noise, making decode without an external LNA impossible regardless of link geometry (\S\ref{sec:packet-reception}). The Friis cascade analysis of the LNA improvement is given in \S\ref{sec:field-tests}.

\section{Doppler Shift}
\label{sec:doppler}

A satellite moving with radial velocity $v_r$ relative to the ground station shifts the received carrier by $\Delta f = v_r \cdot f_0 / c$. The radial velocity reaches its maximum at the geometric horizon, where the full orbital velocity projects onto the line of sight:
\begin{equation}
    v_{r,\max} \approx v = 7669~\text{m/s}.
\end{equation}

At 433~MHz the magnitude of the peak Doppler shift (satellite at horizon, full orbital velocity along the line of sight) is
\begin{equation}
    |\Delta f|_{\max} = \frac{7669}{3 \times 10^8} \cdot 433 \times 10^6 = 11.07~\text{kHz},
\end{equation}
giving a total peak-to-trough excursion of $\pm$11~kHz across a full pass (positive at approach, negative at recession, zero at TCA).

At 145~MHz (representative VHF band centre; no uplink frequency has been defined for AetherSpace at the time of writing):
\begin{equation}
    |\Delta f|_{\max} = \frac{7669}{3 \times 10^8} \cdot 145 \times 10^6 = 3.71~\text{kHz} \quad (\pm 3.7~\text{kHz peak\text{-}to\text{-}trough}).
\end{equation}

The Doppler rate $d(\Delta f)/dt$ peaks at closest approach. The station software applies Doppler correction by writing the CC1200 \code{FREQOFF} register at 2~Hz (\S\ref{sec:pi-stack}). A worst-case rate of approximately 200~Hz/s at 433~MHz near TCA means the residual uncompensated drift between 2~Hz updates is of order 100~Hz, which is small relative to the 10~kHz RX filter bandwidth used for 2.4~kbps 2-GFSK reception.

\section{The SatNOGS Network}
\label{sec:satnogs}

SatNOGS (Satellite Networked Open Ground Station) originated at Hackerspace.gr in Athens during the 2014 NASA Space Apps Challenge and won that year's Hackaday Prize, which funded the Libre Space Foundation~\cite{satnogs-main}. A detailed ecosystem history appears in~\cite{hanssens2023}, which this thesis continues; the summary below is limited to what is load-bearing for the subsequent chapters.

The ecosystem has four components~\cite{satnogs-main, satnogs-network, satnogs-wiki, papadeas2015satnogs}:

\begin{description}
    \item[SatNOGS Network] (\url{network.satnogs.org}): observation scheduling across stations based on TLE pass predictions.
    \item[SatNOGS DB] (\url{db.satnogs.org}): telemetry database, accepts decoded frames over the SiDS (Simple Downlink Sharing) HTTP API.
    \item[SatNOGS Client]: Python daemon running on each station, polls Network for scheduled observations, drives the rotator via hamlib/\code{rotctld}, drives the SDR receiver via SoapySDR + GNU~Radio, uploads results.
    \item[SatNOGS Rotator]: optional reference rotator (3D-printed, NEMA17 bipolar steppers, belt drive, Arduino controller)~\cite{satnogs-rotator-fw}.
\end{description}

Rotator communication goes through hamlib~\cite{hamlib}: the \code{rotctld} daemon exposes a TCP interface on port 4533 speaking the hamlib \code{rotctl} protocol, and internally translates those commands to EasyComm III ASCII on the serial link to the controller. Any controller that speaks EasyComm III integrates without modification, as hamlib model 204~\cite{jackson1990easycomm}.

\subsection{The EasyComm Protocol}

EasyComm is an ASCII protocol developed by Chris Jackson (G7UPN) for antenna tracking~\cite{jackson1990easycomm}. The SatNOGS variant uses hamlib model 204 (EasyComm III), with commands summarised in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

\subsection{Why a Distributed Network}

A single station contacts a LEO satellite for at most $\sim$11\% of each orbit ($t_{\max}/T \approx 10.1/92.4 = 10.9\%$ at 400~km). For mission-critical telemetry, one station is insufficient; distributing stations worldwide gives near-continuous coverage. Earlier attempts (the Japanese Ground Station Network at the University of Tokyo, and ESA's GENSO project, started 2007 and cancelled by 2014) coupled stations through a centralised SOAP-over-SSL software stack, which became the lock-in that prevented open scaling~\cite{hanssens2023}. SatNOGS accepts any hardware that speaks EasyComm over serial and uses an SDR receiver.

\subsection{CC1200 Deviation}
\label{sec:cc1200-deviation}

The standard SatNOGS receive chain is SDR-based. An RTL-SDR v3 dongle feeds raw IQ samples through GNU~Radio flowgraphs (\code{gr-satnogs}) via SoapySDR; demodulation, sync detection, and CRC checking happen in software. This stack is flexible at the cost of compute requirements (GNU~Radio + Docker, targeting $\geq$1~GB of RAM~\cite{satnogs-client-setup}).

This thesis substitutes a CC1200 hardware packet modem for the SDR front-end. The CC1200 performs modulation/demodulation, sync-word detection, and CRC checking in hardware, producing decoded packets rather than raw IQ. As a consequence, the standard \code{satnogs-client} pipeline (built around GNU~Radio) cannot be used as-is; the station submits decoded frames directly to the SatNOGS DB via the SiDS API. The trade-off is reduced modulation flexibility (the CC1200 must be configured to match each satellite's transmit parameters) in exchange for alignment with AetherSpace (which carries a CC1200) and lower compute requirements on the Pi 3A+~\cite{rpi3aplus}.

\section{Comparison with Hanssens (2023)}

This thesis continues the 2023 masterproef by Serge Hanssens~\cite{hanssens2023}, which surveyed the SatNOGS ecosystem, assessed hardware options (rotators, SDRs, antennas), and tested a Stepperdrive MSD-50-5.6D stepper motor driver for azimuth rotation.

Hanssens' load-bearing finding for the present thesis is that the stepper motor he tested did not deliver sufficient torque for reliable azimuth rotation under wind loading~\cite{hanssens2023}. He measured the required torque for azimuth movement on a cantilevered Yagi and concluded that a higher-torque motor or higher gear ratio was needed. This directly informed the choice in the present work of geared DC motors (FAPG36-555-EN, 516:1 internal planetary gearbox) with a further 2.667:1 external reduction, covered in \S\ref{sec:motors}.

Hanssens' research questions were survey-oriented; the present work's are implementation-oriented, and the two studies therefore compare as scope rather than as competing results.
